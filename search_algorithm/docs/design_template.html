Selecting the archive’s name-search instrument: experiment design

Experiment design · 10 October 2026 · evaluation data: extracted_text_old.csv
Summary The experiment selects the search instrument that the archive will operate by comparing candidate generators (cross-script embedding models and lexical phonetic baselines), equivalence dictionaries and ranking functions on queries derived from the transcribed records. A sequential design replaces a full factorial: each of three development experiments varies one component, holds the others at the previous experiment’s best setting and carries the two strongest configurations forward, and a final confirmation run measures the frozen pipeline once on a held-out test split. A profile of the evaluation data shaped the design. Only 38 % of the 473 Spanish–Hebrew given-name pairs on the stones are linked by any phonetic method or by the embedding encoder; most of the remainder share only an initial sound, the pattern of civil names chosen to echo a Hebrew or Yiddish name. Equivalence dictionaries are therefore a primary instrument for given names, and one of them is learned from the archive itself under fold-wise separation.

1Research question

Which search instrument, defined as a candidate generator, an equivalence dictionary and a ranking function, retrieves the intended person most reliably for name queries typed in Latin or Hebrew script, within the latency and memory limits of the Flask service? Three sub-questions follow the components, and each is answered by one development experiment.

  • RQ1 (generators). Which embedding model, and which lexical phonetic method, retrieves the intended person best without any dictionary?
  • RQ2 (dictionaries). How much does an equivalence dictionary add, which dictionary source performs best (curated, archive-derived or both), and where in the pipeline should the dictionary act?
  • RQ3 (ranking). Which ranking function orders the candidates best, and does fusing several generators improve on the best single one?

The primary measure is recall at 10 (R@10), the share of queries whose intended person appears on the first page of results. Mean reciprocal rank (MRR), the number of unrelated people among the first ten results shown above the instrument’s display cut-off, and the 95th-percentile query latency are secondary measures. The selected instrument is the simplest configuration whose R@10 lies within the 95 % confidence interval of the best configuration and whose latency stays under a budget of 500 ms, fixed before the runs begin; simplicity is counted in components.

2Evaluation data

The extraction file holds 828 person rows from 794 graves (628 in the women’s section, 200 in the children’s). Of these, 668 rows record a given name and a surname in at least one script: 587 in Latin script, 444 in Hebrew script and 366 in both. Three properties of the records bear directly on the design.

  • Given names in the two scripts are often different names. Figure 1 classifies the 473 rows that carry a given name in both scripts. In 182 pairs (38 %) the two forms are linked by Daitch–Mokotoff, Beider–Morse or the byte-level encoder (Rebeca–רבקה, Samuel–שמואל). In 191 pairs (40 %) the forms share only their initial consonant class, which covers cognates the methods miss (José–יוסף, Raquel–רחל) and civil names chosen to echo the Hebrew or Yiddish name (Pascual–פסח, Marcos–מרדכי, Cecilia–צירל). The remaining 100 pairs (21 %) share neither, as with translations such as Adolfo or Valentín for זאב (‘wolf’).
  • Married women carry composite surnames. Of 513 Latin-script surnames in the women’s section, 411 contain the married-name particle ‘de’ (‘S. de Blejer’, ‘de Frenkel’), and the Hebrew surname on the same stone may record the maiden name, the married name or both. Surnames must be tokenised, the particle and initials removed, and every surname token indexed.
  • Hebrew script is largely Yiddish orthography. 188 of the 448 Hebrew-script surnames contain ⟨ע⟩ or ⟨טש⟩, the Yiddish spellings of /e/ and /tʃ/ (סערעברינסקי, טאלקאטשיער), which neither Modern Hebrew romanisation nor the embedding models’ training data represent well, and 127 of 583 Hebrew given names are double names (חיה שרה).

2.1 Preparation, splits and query sets

The evaluation set comprises the 657 eligible rows with extraction confidence of at least 85. Rows that describe the same person on two stones (the same names and year of death, as with the Serebrinsky brothers on stones 7 and 8 of the children’s section) are merged into one target with two records, both counted as correct. Names are normalised exactly as the production import will normalise them: case, accents, Hebrew points and final letter forms, the particle ‘de’ and initials. Twenty per cent of persons, stratified by section and by script coverage, form a test split that is touched only in Experiment 4; the other eighty per cent form a development split evaluated by five-fold cross-validation, with all graves of one family surname kept in the same fold so that no surname appears in both a dictionary’s training folds and its evaluation fold.

Queries are generated from the records in five sets (Table 1). The cross-script sets use field masking: for a query typed in one script, the target person’s fields in that script are removed from the index, so that the person can be found only through the other script, which is the situation of the 221 persons recorded in Latin script alone and the 78 recorded in Hebrew script alone.

3Instruments

An instrument is assembled from one entry in each block of Table 2. Lexical baselines are included alongside the embedding models because the literature review found romanise-then-compare pipelines competitive with learned encoders [1], and a claim that one instrument is optimal requires comparison with the cheapest credible alternative.

4Methods

Figure 2 shows the sequence. A preparation stage builds the evaluation set once; Experiments 1–3 then run on the development split; each varies one component, fixes the others at the previous experiment’s best setting and passes its two strongest configurations forward, which bounds the number of runs at about twenty, against several hundred for the full factorial, while keeping a second candidate alive wherever two components might interact. Thresholds, k, λ and the K4 weights are tuned inside the training folds only.

Preparation · Building the evaluation set

  • Apply the eligibility filter and the import’s own normalisation, so that the experiment tests the code path that will run in production.
  • Merge duplicate persons into single targets and record every grave as a correct answer.
  • Draw the test split (20 %, stratified by section and script coverage) and the five development folds, grouping graves by family surname.
  • Generate QS1–QS5 with a fixed random seed; hand-check 50 QS2 variants for plausibility, and generate QS2 with a model other than the one that produced E1’s training pairs, to avoid favouring E1.
  • Report results separately on the rows that fall in the hand-checked transcription sample, where the target names are known to be correct.

Experiment 1 · Candidate generators (RQ1)

  • Index every name field of every development person with each generator, one generator at a time, with no dictionary and maximum-similarity ranking.
  • For embeddings, retrieve with a cut-off and a top-k cap; tune the cut-off on the training folds for R@10 under an unrelated@10 limit.
  • Compare generators on R@10 per query set with paired bootstrap confidence intervals [6]; carry forward the best two embedding models and the best lexical baseline.
  • If E4 is run, fine-tune within each fold on that fold’s training pairs only.

Experiment 2 · Dictionary source and placement (RQ2)

  • Fix the generators from Experiment 1 and compare three placements of the dictionary: dictionary only (P1), dictionary before the embedding (P2), and embedding before the dictionary (P3, the pipeline proposed on 10 October), each with D1, D2 and D3 against D0.
  • For P3, test the two settings that moved results most in the October simulation: searching the archive’s spellings alone against the archive’s spellings plus the dictionary’s, and a similarity cut-off against a fixed top-k.
  • Link every archive spelling to its dictionary group at import (exact entry, else nearest dictionary spelling above a cut-off); for D2 this happens per fold. Record the share of links a reviewer would reject on a sample of 100.
  • Report QS3 and QS4 separately for given names and surnames, since the dictionaries matter mainly for given names (Figure 1), and carry forward the best two configurations.

Experiment 3 · Ranking and fusion (RQ3)

  • Pool the candidates of the Experiment 2 configurations with those of the best lexical baseline and score each person with K1–K4.
  • Aggregate the given-name and surname components per person and compare a sum with a product, which penalises a strong match on one part alone.
  • Fit λ (K2) and the K4 weights on the training folds; inspect the K4 coefficients to see which signals carry weight, including the D4 initial-sound feature.
  • Select the pipeline to freeze by the decision rule of §1 and record its full configuration before opening the test split.

Experiment 4 · Confirmation on the test split

  • Run the frozen pipeline once on the test queries, with dictionaries D2 rebuilt from the whole development split, and report every metric with 95 % bootstrap intervals, alongside the current design (L3 with K1) as the reference.
  • Compare the selected pipeline with the runner-up on R@10 with McNemar’s test for paired outcomes [7].
  • Break results down by query set, section, script direction, name length and extraction confidence, and examine every QS3–QS4 failure by hand, sorting each into a missing equivalence, a transcription error or a ranking error.
  • Measure latency on CPU at the Render instance size (cold and warm), including query embedding and the PostgreSQL lookups.

5Threats to validity

The targets come from automatic extraction, so some intended names are themselves misread (שמרן for שמואל on one Serebrinsky stone); the hand-checked subset bounds this effect. QS2 relies on generated spellings, which may resemble E1’s LLM-generated training data, and the hand check and the use of a different generating model mitigate but do not remove that bias. The archive-derived dictionary is learned from the evaluation data, and fold-wise construction is what keeps its gains honest. With about 130 test persons the intervals will be wide, and differences of a few points of R@10 should be read as ties. The file evaluated is the older extraction; the protocol should be rerun unchanged on the final transcription before deployment.
